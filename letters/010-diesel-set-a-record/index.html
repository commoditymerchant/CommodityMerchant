Diesel set a record the same week crude fell.

The barrel eased. The part of it that moves trucks did not — and the strait counted eight ships through all of it.

TRADING ECONOMICS · HEATING OIL PAGE 20 SEP · BRENT PAGE 19 SEP 2026 · INDICATIVE · NOT ADVICE

Brent fell for three sessions in a row and finished the week down 1.2%. If you only watched crude, it was a week of relief — a Saudi pipeline repair timetable, talk of alternative routes, a risk premium leaking out.

In the same five days, the US diesel benchmark set a record.

Heating oil, the contract that prices American diesel, reached $5.26 a gallon earlier in the week before easing to around $5.06. Over twelve months it is up 120%. Brent, over the same twelve months, is up 56%.

Same barrel. Two very different weeks. Here is why, from the physical side.

02 / The Barrel Split In Two

Crude is a raw material. Nobody burns it. What the world actually buys is what a refinery makes from it — diesel for trucks, ships, tractors and generators; gasoline for cars; jet fuel. The price gap between crude and those products is called the crack spread, and it is the refinery’s margin for doing the cracking.

When crude and products move together, the crack spread barely changes and nobody writes about it. When they come apart, the market is telling you where the bottleneck is. This week they came apart as widely as they have all year.

Brent settled around $103.87 on Friday, and its own page is plain about the direction: "Brent crude prices tumbled to $103 a barrel on Friday, erasing gains after a volatile session and falling for a third consecutive session."

The heating-oil page, dated today, puts it this way: "US heating oil prices fell to around $5.04 per gallon, retreating from a record high of $5.26 reached earlier this week, as rising inventories and expectations of alternative routes for Middle Eastern barrels offered some respite from tight distillate supplies."

Read that last phrase again: tight distillate supplies. The Gulf does not just export crude. It exports refined products, and it exports them into a world that was already short of refining capacity. You can reroute a crude cargo around a closed strait with enough ships and enough money. You cannot conjure a refinery.

THE PHYSICAL READ

When crude eases and diesel sets a record, the shortage isn’t oil. It’s the machinery that turns oil into something you can use.

Gasoline sits in between — $3.53 a gallon, up 79% on the year, and its page says it eased from an eight-week high on expectations that Saudi Arabia could restore some disrupted exports. It is the same pattern at lower volume: the further along the barrel you go, the tighter it gets.

03 / The Water

Day 202. The tracker’s latest published transit day, 13 September, shows eight vessels against a normal of roughly 85 a day — about 9% of normal. 416 ships are holding position. War-risk cover runs 40 times normal, about $10 million per VLCC transit, and the same six P&I clubs remain withdrawn.

The talks did not happen. The meeting that sent twenty-five markets lower the Friday before last was postponed on the Sunday night with no new date. Al Jazeera’s headline on 14 September: "Temporary Hormuz solution deferred as Iran-Arab summit falls through."

What was on the table matters more than the postponement. The draft set out temporary entry and exit routes for commercial vessels, and could have included "voluntary tolls to fund navigation, environmental protection and search-and-rescue operations." Last week’s letter called the toll the most consequential detail nobody was putting in a headline. It is now the detail the meeting stalled on — the reporting says Riyadh objected to wording it feared would "establish a new status quo."

The bypass is still shut. Saudi Arabia’s East–West pipeline to the Red Sea, normally 4 to 5 million barrels a day, remained offline on 18 September. The operator is working to restore about half its capacity within days and full capacity in about six weeks.

So the barrels found another way. Reuters, relayed by OilPrice on 18 September, reports that Saudi Arabia has sold "as many as 60 million barrels" of crude from Ras Tanura for September and October loadings via ship-to-ship transfers. Tanker rates were reported near $1 million a day earlier in the week.

THE SEQUENCE

A pipe goes down → cargoes move to ships → ships cost a million a day → the transit count still reads eight. Barrels can find a way around a chokepoint. The count only moves when insurers do.

The tracker’s own reopening odds: 1% by 30 September, 5% by 1 November, 18% by 31 December, 45% by 1 July 2027.

04 / The Metal

Copper’s squeeze ended on Monday and restarted on Friday. On 14 September the LME cash price settled below three-month metal for the first time in the run — contango, the shape of a comfortable market. It stayed there for four sessions. On 18 September cash settled at $14,529.00 against three-month $14,515.00 — a $14.00 backwardation again, derived from the two printed legs. Small, but the sign flipped back.

The price agreed. Cash is up $290.50, or 2.0%, on the week, and Trading Economics has COMEX copper up for a fourth straight session at $6.62 a pound, "supported by signs of stronger demand from China."

And yet the metal kept arriving. LME stocks are 255,100 tonnes, up 20,625 tonnes on the week — the sheds are filling while the curve tightens. That combination is unusual and worth watching: it usually means the metal arriving is not the metal the market wants, or not where it wants it.

Underneath the tape, the supply side got a sober read on 18 September. Sprott, reported by MINING.COM, says global copper mine supply risks its first decline since 2017: output down 1.1% year-on-year in the first half, disruptions removing roughly 600,000 tonnes — about 2.5% of expected 2026 supply — and US cathode imports hitting a record 223,000 tonnes in July.

Aluminium settled at $3,285.00 cash against $3,282.00 three-month — a $3.00 backwardation, down from $24 a week ago and now almost flat. Stock 242,600 tonnes. Trading Economics’ page commentary notes Gulf production running 44% below a year earlier in July, from a region that made around a tenth of the world’s aluminium before the conflict.

Zinc is still the steepest curve on the exchange — $97.00 backwardation (cash $4,010.00, three-month $3,913.00) — but it has narrowed from $143 last week while stocks rose 5,725 tonnes to 115,300. The squeeze I flagged last week loosened.

Alumina: the only dated print this week is Chinese spot at RMB 2,550–2,700 a tonne for the week ending 10 September (AL Circle, published 15 September), with about half the industry reported to be running at a full-cost loss. There is no dated FOB Australia print, so the board row carries the Chinese range, labelled.

05 / The Metal That Ignored The Fed

The Federal Reserve raised rates by 25 basis points on Wednesday and signalled more. That should be bad for gold — a non-yielding asset competing against a higher rate.

Gold closed Friday at $4,383.45, up 0.97%, at a one-week high, and its page records its first weekly gain in four weeks. Silver finished at $66.24, up 1.60%, its highest in more than a week.

Both pages give the same reason, and it connects straight back to section 02: "falling oil prices eased concerns over prolonged inflationary pressures." The market is treating oil as the inflation input and gold as the thing that reacts to it. When crude eased, the pressure on rates eased with it, and gold took that as permission.

The rest of the precious complex didn’t follow. Palladium sits near $1,319, around its lowest in over a month; platinum near $1,806, near two-week lows. Gold and silver are trading the macro; the platinum group is trading its own industrial story.

06 / The Molecule And The Grid

TTF ended at €79.52 a megawatt hour, up 4.14% on Friday but down around 2% on the week — its first weekly drop in six. It is still up 146% over twelve months, the largest yearly move on the board. Its page gives the reason in one line: "depleted reserves and disruptions to LNG shipments remaining in focus."

The latest EU storage read I could open is still 68.04% full as of 13 September, refilling at about 0.21 percentage points a day toward a projected ~78% by 1 November. The tracker had not published a newer fill figure by Sunday morning, so that number is a week old and labelled as such.

Coal is the quiet tell. At around $144 a tonne it sits near three-month highs, and its page cites the IEA: disruption has pushed oil and gas prices higher, "encouraging a shift toward coal-fired power generation." When gas gets expensive enough, power stations switch fuel. The coal price is the cost of that switch.

And the grid keeps asking for more. Gartner’s June forecast has data-centre electricity use rising 26% this year, from 447 to 565 terawatt-hours, and passing 1,200 by 2030. The September EIA outlook, as reported by Reuters, forecasts record US power demand in both 2026 and 2027. Every one of those terawatt-hours is gas, coal, uranium, wind or sun — and copper wire. AI is not a commodity. It is a very large new customer for several of them.

07 / Watchlist — what I’m watching from here
01

The crack spread, not the crude price. If diesel stays near its record while Brent drifts, the bottleneck is refining, and it won’t be fixed by any agreement about a strait.

02

The pipeline’s first half. "Half within days" was said on 16 and 17 September. When it actually flows, the ship-to-ship workaround and the million-dollar tanker day both get cheaper.

03

A new date for the talks — and the toll. The lane is drafted. The toll is the argument. Watch which one moves first.

04

Copper’s curve against copper’s sheds. Backwardation with rising stocks can’t last long. One of them gives.

05

Gold’s link to oil. This week gold rallied through a rate hike because oil fell. If oil turns, that support goes with it.

Crude fell, diesel set a record, gold ignored a rate hike, copper tightened while its warehouses filled, and the strait counted eight ships. None of those contradict each other. They are the same week seen from different points on the supply chain — and the closer you get to the thing a customer actually uses, the tighter it gets.

The metal always moves. See you Sunday.

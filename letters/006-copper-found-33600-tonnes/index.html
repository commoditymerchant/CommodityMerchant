Copper found 33,600 tonnes. Zinc found none.

One warehouse system, one week, three base metals and three completely different answers about whether metal is scarce. The squeeze didn’t end. It changed address.

01 / The Board

The week on the board

Energy did the shouting again — Brent’s second consecutive week up more than five per cent, European gas up six — but the interesting story was quieter and sat in the metals. Three base metals traded on the same exchange, under the same warehouse rules, in the same five sessions, and every one of them said something different about supply.

02 / The Unwind

Copper’s squeeze died in five sessions

On Monday, copper cash settled $535 over the three-month. That is a market saying, loudly, that it wants metal now and will pay a fat premium to skip the queue. By Friday the same spread was $56. Nine-tenths of it gone in a working week.

Nothing mystical happened. Somebody opened warehouse doors. LME copper stocks bottomed at 204,975 tonnes on 14 August and closed Friday at 238,575 — 33,600 tonnes, a 16.4% build, in five sessions. When metal arrives, the premium for prompt delivery has nothing left to price.

That is the difference between a shortage and a bottleneck. A shortage means the metal is not there. A bottleneck means the metal is there and something — freight, paperwork, a tariff wall, an incentive to sit off-warrant — is keeping it from where the contract needs it to be. Bottlenecks resolve in days once the arithmetic tips. Shortages don’t.

The Physical Read

A backwardation that collapses on deliveries was never about scarcity. It was about location.

03 / The Other Direction

Zinc tightened while everyone watched copper

In the same week, on the same exchange, zinc went the other way. Its cash premium over three-month widened to $156 on Friday, and its warehouse stock fell to 93,125 tonnes, still draining. Copper found 33,600 tonnes when the price begged. Zinc kept raising the offer and nobody came.

Scale matters here. Zinc’s entire visible LME stock is smaller than the amount of copper that turned up in one week. Put lead next to it for contrast: 416,850 tonnes in the sheds and a $41 contango — four and a half times zinc’s inventory, and a curve begging you to hold it rather than deliver it. Same exchange, same warehouse network, opposite worlds.

And then there is aluminium, which did nothing at all. Its published stock figure read 246,925 tonnes on all five settles, after falling about 10.8% over the previous month. While the pile sat still, the curve widened from a $1 contango on Thursday to $17 on Friday — the back end rising faster than the front. A market that needed metal today would not pay you to keep it.

Which is where a definition earns its keep. Exchange stock counts metal on warrant — registered with the exchange. Metal in the same shed that is not warranted does not appear in the figure everyone quotes. So a falling number can mean three quite different things: metal consumed, metal relocated, or metal simply de-registered. Only one of those is a shortage, and the headline number will not tell you which.

The Sequence

Price begs → metal appears → premium dies. If the middle step never happens, you are not looking at a bottleneck any more.

04 / The Molecule Behind The Metal

Gas is an aluminium story now

European gas closed the week at €65.87, up six per cent and roughly double where it stood a year ago. The blockade through Hormuz has halted Qatari cargoes; European buyers are competing with Asian ones; storage sat at 61.11% on 17 August against a five-year seasonal norm nearer 67.5%. The European Commission says supply is not immediately at risk. Storage is a number you can only fix in advance, and August is late to start.

Where it gets interesting is two steps downstream. Smelting aluminium needs alumina; refining alumina needs gas. Alunorte — one of the largest alumina refineries in the world — has been running at half capacity on gas shortages, with the same conflict disrupting the LNG that refining depends on. That is a gas problem arriving in the metals complex wearing a different hat.

Do the arithmetic on a tonne of aluminium and it stops being abstract. It takes roughly 1.93 tonnes of alumina to make a tonne of primary metal. At the $346 LME/Platts print, that is about $668 of ore inside a $3,227 tonne — call it a fifth. The other four-fifths is power, carbon, freight and time. Aluminium is not really a rock business. It is an energy business that ships in ingot form.

Oil, meanwhile, keeps climbing on the same map. Brent finished at $94.39 and put in a second straight week above five per cent, up nearly forty per cent on the year. The Strait is on day 175 of closure — one transit on the last reported day against roughly seventy-three a day before the crisis, war-risk cover at forty times pre-crisis, six P&I clubs withdrawn. One number did move in the right direction: the queue of ships holding away from berth fell from 365 to 337. It is the first meaningful fall since this began, and it is a single day’s reading, so treat it as a thing to watch rather than a turn.

05 / The Metals With No Warehouse Problem

Gold and silver had a good week for a dull reason

Gold closed at $4,607.35, its highest since mid-May and around five per cent up on the week. The move came from the Treasury unexpectedly increasing its purchases of longer-dated debt, which pushed yields and the dollar down and reopened the fiscal-sustainability argument. Gold does not need a warrant, a shed or a freight rate. That is precisely what people are buying.

Silver finished at $68.95 — a third consecutive weekly gain, and up more than seventy-seven per cent on the year. It got the same yield tailwind, plus an industrial one: Chinese solar and grid demand. Silver is the one precious metal that gets consumed rather than stored, which is why it behaves like a hybrid and why it has run harder than gold all year.

The Physical Read

Base metals price the distance between metal and contract. Precious metals price the distance between money and trust. Both were wide this week.

06 / Watchlist

What I’m watching from here

01
Whether aluminium’s figure ever moves.

Five identical settles is unusual. The tell will be which way it breaks — a build says the metal was always there, a fresh draw with the curve still in contango says the number and the market have stopped agreeing.

02
Zinc’s deliveries, or the absence of them.

Copper’s premium died the moment metal showed up. If zinc holds a triple-digit backwardation into another week with no arrivals, the bottleneck reading stops being available.

03
The ships holding at Hormuz.

365 to 337 in a day. One reading is noise; three in the same direction would be the first physical sign of anything changing at the chokepoint, and it would show up in freight and war-risk long before it showed up in flat price.

04
Jackson Hole, 27–29 August.

With gold rallying on a debt-buyback surprise and yields the swing factor, the keynote on the Friday is the week’s obvious macro event. Watch what it does to real yields, not to the headline.

The metal always moves. See you Sunday.

There were two wars on the board this week, and the market spent the week pricing them in opposite directions. In one, a head of state said the word peace out loud and the grain complex sold off. In the other, four ships crossed a strait that used to carry a hundred and thirty-eight a day.

Only one of those facts changed anything that floats. Here is the week from the physical side.

02 / The Sentence

A sentence at a conference took 2.72% out of wheat

On Thursday, at an economic forum in Russia’s Far East, President Vladimir Putin was reported to have said there was “a chance of a peace agreement in Ukraine.” By Friday’s print wheat was at $716.00 a bushel, down 2.72% on the day, easing — in Trading Economics’ own words — “from over a three-year high, amid expectations of a peace agreement between Russia and Ukraine.”

Nothing had been signed. Nothing had been loaded. No vessel changed course. A sentence was spoken at a conference and roughly two and a half per cent came out of the price of bread wheat.

This is not a criticism of the market. It is the market doing exactly its job. What moved was the war premium — the part of a price that pays for the possibility of disruption rather than the disruption itself. War premium is the cheapest thing on any commodity board to add and the cheapest thing to remove, because it is not attached to anything you can weigh. It leaves the moment the possibility recedes.

The physical read

A headline moves the price. A season moves the tonnage. Both are real; only one of them loads a ship.

And the same report carried the caution that mattered: traders warned that the remarks “did not indicate an immediate reduction in military activity and that physical disruptions to Black Sea grain shipments remained unchanged.”

03 / The Tonnage

It had already halved, and nobody led on it

Here is the number that did not make the headline. Ukraine’s combined grain and oilseed exports fell to 1.2 million tonnes in August, from 2.5 million tonnes in July — the figure comes from the Ukrainian traders union UGA, reported the same day as the peace remark. That is a fall of roughly 52%, derived from the two printed tonnages.

So on the day the price came down on the prospect of peace, the actual flow of grain out of the country had already halved.

And the market could not make up its mind about it either. On the same morning, from the same publisher, two pages said opposite things about the same sea:

  • The wheat page: prices easing “amid expectations of a peace agreement.”
  • The corn page: futures “trading near their highest level in more than three years, as concerns over disruptions to Black Sea grain exports added support ahead of the region’s peak corn export season.”

One page is pricing the war ending. The other is pricing the war continuing. Both were written on the same day about the same water. Soybeans, meanwhile, held above $13 near their highest since December 2023, on Chinese buying rather than on anything geopolitical at all.

That disagreement is not sloppiness. It is what a market looks like when the paper is trading a headline and the physical is trading a harvest calendar. They are on different clocks.

04 / The Other War

Four ships, and a tanker hit on Saturday morning

Nothing in the Gulf softened. Brent finished the week at $96.28 a barrel, and the page’s own commentary put it “up more than 8% for the week, marking its strongest weekly performance since July, as the war in the Middle East continued with little prospects of peace.”

The transit numbers underneath that price are the part worth keeping. Kpler counted four commodity vessels through the Strait of Hormuz on 3 September, down from nine the day before, against a pre-war daily average of roughly 138. Independent tracking reported by the Joint Maritime Information Center showed “single-digit numbers transiting in both directions.”

A note on that baseline: the Hormuz tracker at straits.live prints a peacetime figure of about 85 a day; the GlobalSecurity update prints about 138. Both are noted here; neither is reconciled, and no arithmetic in this letter depends on which is right.

And the week’s freshest beat arrived on Saturday morning. Iranian media reported that four U.S. missiles struck an Iranian tanker at the Kharg Island anchorage, with no casualties reported. CENTCOM had not confirmed the strike at the time of that report. Kharg is not an incidental target: it handles the large majority of Iran’s crude exports.

Gas is where that shows up in a European household bill. TTF sat at €71.96 a megawatt hour, up 125.08% over twelve months — the largest yearly move on anything verified this week — with the page describing prices rising “above €72 per MWh on Friday… as investors assessed renewed tensions in the Middle East,” and European inventories below normal heading into winter while Gulf LNG shipments run late.

The sequence

Chokepoint closes → LNG cargoes lengthen or cancel → European storage refills slower than the calendar needs → the winter curve does the arguing. The price is the last step, not the first.

05 / The Metals Kept Their Own Counsel

An $84 backwardation, a fifty-cent contango and a refinery at half rate

Copper settled at $14,359.00 a tonne cash on the LME on 3 September, with three-month at $14,275.00 — an $84.00 backwardation, derived from the two printed legs. Exchange stock stood at 234,650 tonnes, up 800 on the day. On the COMEX side the same metal printed $6.58 a pound on 5 September, which converts to roughly $14,506 a tonne — a gap of about $150 between two venues for the same red metal, on two different dates. The split market has not healed.

Aluminium settled at $3,303.00 cash against $3,303.50 three-month — a fifty-cent contango, which is another way of saying no view at all. The interesting part was underneath: the Trading Economics page reported LME inventories “near a 36-year low” and Shanghai inventories down 3% on the week, and noted that Alunorte in Brazil — the world’s largest alumina refinery outside China — had run at 50% capacity in August before ramping back to full after securing a temporary gas supply agreement.

Read that sentence again and notice what it is actually about. The world’s biggest alumina refinery outside China had its output halved for a month by a gas problem. The molecule sits upstream of the metal, always.

Alumina itself has no row on this week’s board. No dated public print could be verified on Sunday morning, so the row is blank rather than carried forward from last week. A stale number is worse than a missing one.

Gold and silver both took the same instruction and read it the same way. Gold at $4,432.56 an ounce, silver at $65.99, both down on the session after August US non-farm payrolls came in at 162,000 against expectations near 56,000, unemployment steady at 4.1%, and money-market pricing moved back toward a September Federal Reserve hike. Over twelve months, though, silver is up 61.11% and gold 23.38% — the industrial half of the precious pair is still winning by a distance.

And in the background, the sector’s paper valuation has gone vertical: MINING.COM’s own headline on 4 September recorded the value of the top 50 mining companies rising $357 billion in August, the largest monthly gain on record, taking the group above $2.5 trillion for the first time since February.

06 / The Grid Under All Of It

The demand story nobody votes on

Brookings, in a piece updated 10 April 2026, collects the numbers that matter for anyone trying to work out where the next decade of electricity, copper and gas demand comes from:

  • Global data-centre electricity consumption was about 415 terawatt hours in 2024 — roughly 1.5% of the world’s total electricity use — citing the IEA.
  • That figure has grown at a 12% compound annual rate since 2017, more than four times faster than total global electricity consumption.
  • The IEA’s base case has it reaching 945 TWh by 2030 and 1,200 TWh by 2035.
  • In the United States, the Department of Energy’s 2024 Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory report found data centres consumed about 4.4% of total US electricity in 2023, projected to reach 6.7% to 12.0% by 2028.
  • Ireland is the cautionary tale: data centres and other large energy users were 21% of national electricity demand in 2023.

None of that moved a price this week. All of it is the reason the gas, copper and power stories on this board keep refusing to mean-revert.

07 / Watchlist

What I’m watching from here

1

The tonnage, not the talk. September’s Ukrainian export figure, when UGA publishes it. If the peace premium came out of the price in a day and the September tonnage comes in near August’s 1.2 Mt, the price was early. If it recovers toward July’s 2.5 Mt, it was right.

2

Whether Kharg changes the transit count. A strike on a tanker at anchorage is a different signal from a strike on a terminal. Watch the daily commodity-vessel count through Hormuz, not the statements.

3

European storage against the calendar. TTF up 125% over twelve months with inventories described as below normal is a winter problem being priced in September. The refill rate is the number.

4

Alumina getting a print again. Alunorte at half capacity for a month should show up somewhere in the alumina index. It is the cleanest read on whether the aluminium cost curve is about to move.

Wheat spent Thursday and Friday unwinding eleven months of war premium on the strength of one sentence, and it is still up 42.91% over twelve months. That is the shape of the whole board right now: enormous yearly numbers, jumpy daily ones, and underneath both a set of ports, straits and refineries that move at their own speed and are entirely indifferent to what anybody says at a conference.

Paper reprices in an afternoon. The port does not.

The metal always moves. See you Sunday.

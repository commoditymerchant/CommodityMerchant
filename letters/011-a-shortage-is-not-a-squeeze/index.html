The Merchant's Wrap · No. 011 Sunday 27 September 2026 Week ending 26 September

Nickel warehouses took in 6,048 tonnes the week production was cut.

Three days after smelters at Indonesia's largest nickel park said they were cutting output, the price fell and the warehouses got fuller. That is not a market ignoring the news — it is a market counting what is already in the shed, and deciding the news is worth about ten days.

Two energy benchmarks fell on the same peace headline. Two precious metals sat pinned under Treasury yields. And two base metals, both carrying a live supply disruption, told completely opposite stories — which is the whole letter.

02 / The Metal — nickel, and the cushion nobody quoted

A cut landed on the deepest shed on the board.

The headline was real. On 22 September, smelters at Indonesia's largest nickel industrial park cut nickel pig iron output. The reason given was a water shortage tied to this year's El Niño. Indonesia is the single most important source of nickel units in the world, so this is not a footnote.

The tape's answer, three days later, was a shrug. Nickel closed the 25 September session at $16,359.75 a tonne, down 0.88%. On that same settle, LME warehouses took in another 6,048 tonnes, lifting visible stock to 284,946. The shed grew 2.2% in one session — roughly 62% of a single day's global consumption — on the very day the disruption was supposed to bite.

The number that explains it never made the headline. Reported inventory is described on the nickel page as around 478,000 tonnes, or roughly seven weeks of global consumption. Work that through and you get about 9,755 tonnes a day. The cut being quoted in market commentary — around 100,000 tonnes — is therefore worth about ten days. The cover is about forty-nine. Even if every tonne of the quoted cut vanished, the stock covers it five times over.

The Physical Read

A shortage is not a squeeze. A cut in output moves the price only when there is nothing between it and the consumer. Check the shed before you check the headline.

And here is the part worth keeping. The 100,000-tonne figure doing all the work in the write-ups is a market estimate, not a disclosure. The park operator's own statement, as reported on 22 September, was that it "has not received exact figures from tenants on the extent of the production cuts." The people doing the cutting have not said how much. Everyone quoting the tonnage is quoting someone's guess, and the arithmetic built on top of it inherits every bit of that uncertainty. Say which number you used.

03 / The Other Metal — copper, same headline, no cushion

Forty-two per cent against eight.

Copper is carrying a live disruption too. Operations remain suspended at a major Chilean mine, and Chinese refinery maintenance runs into the fourth quarter. Copper's own price page also names demand from data centres and energy storage pulling from the other side.

The tape treats it completely differently. Copper is up 42.12% over twelve months. Nickel is up 7.95%. Same class of news, opposite price behaviour, and the variable is not the quality of the headline — it is what sits between the disruption and the consumer.

This is the honest version of the AI-demand story, incidentally. Data-centre build-out does not bid for "metals". It bids for the specific metal where the cushion is thin, and it shows up in the price there first. On this week's board that is copper, and it is not nickel.

04 / The Molecule — a proposal, and two benchmarks that believed it

Gas fell four per cent into a tightening physical position.

European gas fell 4.07% to €71.89 a megawatt-hour on 25 September, attributed on its own page to discussions between the United States and Iran over the Strait of Hormuz. The same page notes storage sitting around 70% full, still below the five-year seasonal average and the region's 80% target, with Norwegian pipeline maintenance and disrupted Gulf LNG both pulling the other way into winter. A market falling 4% into a tightening physical position is worth sitting with.

Brent fell 2.14% to $104.32, on the same diplomatic reporting. WTI fell 2.33% to $92.41, leaving an $11.91 spread — and the reason being floated on the wire is talk of a United States restriction on diesel exports. That mechanism is worth stating plainly: restrict where the refined barrel can go, and you strand the crude behind it. The discount is that constraint, priced before it exists.

The Sequence

An announcement is a forecast. A settlement is a measurement. A transit count is a measurement of something else again. This week the paper markets priced the first, and neither of the other two moved.

05 / The Water — day 209, and an insurance market that did not blink

The cover is the cleaner read.

The strait entered the week still closed to commercial shipping. The most recent transit figure published for it was one vessel, on 20 September, against a pre-crisis baseline of roughly 85 a day. War-risk cover stood at 40 times pre-crisis levels, around $10 million per VLCC transit, with six P&I clubs withdrawn and Cape of Good Hope surcharges of $600 to $1,200 per TEU across nine carriers, all in place since March.

Gas and oil both repriced on a proposal. The insurance market repriced nothing at all. Underwriters re-rate after transits resume, not when a document is tabled — which makes the cover, not the crude, the cleaner read on what the shipping industry actually expects.

On the capacity side, the Saudi East–West bypass pipeline restarted on 22 September and is building volume toward roughly 4 million barrels a day against a 7 million maximum, with full restoration projected at six to eight weeks. Loadings at the receiving terminal had not resumed as of 24 September.

06 / The Metals That Answer To Rates

Neither is trading on anything physical.

Gold closed at $4,285.00 an ounce, up 0.24% on the day and down more than 1% on the week. Silver closed at $64.26, up 0.68% on the day and down more than 3% on the week. Both pages give the same reason: elevated Treasury yields and hardening expectations of further tightening, with roughly a 70% probability priced for an October rate rise and about 95% for December. Ten- and thirty-year yields are at their highest since 2007 and 2004 respectively.

Silver's twelve-month gain of 39.48% still towers over gold's 13.70%, which is the industrial half of silver doing work that gold has no equivalent for. But week to week, both are trading as rates instruments, and neither is trading on anything physical.

07 / Watchlist

What I'm watching from here.

  1. Whether the nickel operator ever publishes a tonnage. If a real figure lands and it is materially above 100,000 tonnes, the days-of-cover arithmetic in section 02 moves and the shrug stops being justified. If no figure ever lands, that is worth noticing too.
  2. Whether LME nickel stock keeps building. One session of +6,048 tonnes is an observation. Four consecutive weeks of it while a disruption is live would be a statement about demand, not supply.
  3. The Brent–WTI spread against the diesel-ban talk. If the spread widens without the policy arriving, the market is pricing an expectation. If it closes on a denial, we will know exactly what that expectation was worth — the same test the copper tariff premium failed earlier this month.
  4. The transit count, not the communiqué. A seven-day proposal was tabled on 25 September. The measurement that settles it is how many ships go through, and when.

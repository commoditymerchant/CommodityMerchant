02 / The Metal

The warehouses filled, and the premium did not care

Start with the quantity. On the LME table dated 30 October, copper stocks in the exchange's warehouse system stood at 274,000 tonnes. On the table dated 2 November they stood at 293,775 — a jump of 19,775 tonnes, 7.2 per cent, in a single session. The following day Reuters reported the total at 309,650 tonnes, a four-month high, with 17,325 tonnes of that day's inflow going into warehouses registered in the United States.

Read that on its own and you have a market loosening. More metal, visible, sitting in sheds the exchange itself certifies.

Now the price of time. On the same 2 November table, copper cash settled at $14,380 a tonne against $14,242 for three-month delivery. Cash above forward is backwardation, and it means the market is paying $138 a tonne for the privilege of having the metal now rather than in ninety days. On the 30 October table that spread had been $199. It narrowed. It did not go.

This is the part that should stop you. A build of nearly twenty thousand tonnes in one session is precisely the event that is supposed to kill a backwardation, because the entire justification for paying up for prompt metal is that prompt metal is scarce. Twenty thousand tonnes arrived and the market still paid.

The Physical Read

A stock total is a global number. A buyer is never a global buyer. When the total goes up and the premium stays, the metal has moved — it has just not moved to anybody who needed it.

And then the third number, which settles it. On 3 November the Yangshan import premium — what a buyer pays over the exchange price to land refined copper in China — sat near $200 a tonne, its highest in more than a decade.

Put the three beside each other and the contradiction dissolves. Copper went into American sheds. Chinese buyers paid the biggest premium in ten years. The exchange went on charging $138 for immediacy. Nobody is confused; the metal is simply in the wrong place, and every one of those numbers is telling you so in a different dialect.

A tonne in Baltimore is not available to a cable plant outside Ningbo at any price that survives the freight, the insurance and the customs clearance. That sentence is most of the physical trade, and it is the sentence a stock total cannot say.

03 / The Curve

Four of the six are paying up

The structure says the same thing across the complex. Cash minus three-month on the 2 November table, metal by metal: tin +$170, copper +$138, zinc +$74, aluminium +$35 — all backwardated, all paying for immediacy. Against them, nickel at −$240 and lead at −$32.50, both in contango, both charging you to wait.

Tin is the one worth a second look, and long-time readers will know why: it is the smallest shed on the exchange. Stock has not moved at all — 4,030 tonnes, unchanged across both tables. The spread has: $95 on 30 October, $170 on 2 November. Four thousand tonnes is a rounding error for a market that size, and a cash market paying up on a pile that small is not a supply story so much as a seating arrangement. There are more people who want a chair than there are chairs.

The Sequence
  1. Metal arrives in the warehouse system, and the headline total rises.
  2. The cash-to-forward spread narrows — because some of that metal is genuinely available.
  3. If the spread stops narrowing while the total keeps rising, the remainder is not available to the people bidding.
  4. At that point the regional premium, not the exchange price, is the number that tells you the truth.
04 / The Molecule

Europe has started taking gas out

The gas story turned while the letter was quiet, and it turned in the direction that matters. European storage read 74.89 per cent on 29 October, 74.62 per cent on 1 November and 74.56 per cent on 2 November. Three readings, three declines. Injection season is over; Europe is now drawing down.

The level is not the story — three-quarters full going into November is not a crisis. The direction is. From here every reading is a subtraction, and the only questions are how cold it gets and how much LNG turns up. Germany sat at 61.05 per cent on 2 November, against a government target of 70, which means the continent's largest consumer entered withdrawal season nine points behind its own stated plan.

TTF itself is doing nothing dramatic: €93.69 on the Nov/03 field, down 1.23 per cent on the day. But it is up 27.45 per cent on the month and 187.83 per cent on the year, and that year-on-year figure is the one to carry around. Gas is not cheap; it is merely not moving today.

05 / The Other Half

Gold's year has gone negative. Silver's has not.

Here is the cleanest thing on the board. Gold's year-on-year field reads −1.13 per cent. It was positive as recently as the day before. An ounce of gold is now worth marginally less than it was twelve months ago.

Silver's year-on-year field reads +3.62 per cent — after a month of −18.58 per cent, which is more than three and a half times gold's monthly fall of 4.94 per cent.

So which metal had the worse month? In percentage points, silver, and it is not close. But run the same month as a share of what each metal had actually gained, and the ranking inverts. Silver gave back about 87 per cent of the year it had built. Gold gave back about 128 per cent — more than all of it, which is precisely why its year crossed zero.

Neither answer is wrong. They are different units. Points are what a headline quotes; shares are what risk is actually carried in. A small gain is fragile for the same reason a thin pile of tin is: there is very little of it to give back.

06 / The Machine

The same equity move, showing on three tapes

One connective thread is worth naming. On 3 November the gold, silver and copper pages all attributed the session to the same thing: a global selloff in technology and artificial-intelligence equities. Three different markets, one move showing through from somewhere else entirely.

It cuts both ways, and the longer horizon points the other direction. S&P Global's published forecast has copper demand rising by half — from 28 million tonnes in 2025 to 42 million by 2040 — and warns that without serious investment supply could fall ten million tonnes short. The International Energy Agency expects data-centre electricity consumption to roughly double, to about 950 terawatt-hours by 2030, near 3 per cent of world demand. Both figures were published in August; both are about wires and transformers as much as about chips.

So AI sells copper off on a bad Tuesday and bids it up over fifteen years. Which of those you are looking at is a choice of window, and that choice is doing more work than most people admit.

07 / Watchlist

What I'm watching from here

  1. Whether copper's backwardation finally breaks. It went $199 to $138 across one session's build. If the total keeps climbing and the spread flips into contango, the squeeze is genuinely over. If the total climbs and the spread holds, it never was a supply story.
  2. The Yangshan premium. A decade high is a decade high. It either pulls metal east over the coming weeks — in which case the LME total stops rising — or it does not, and we learn something uncomfortable about what is actually free to move.
  3. European storage, now that it only goes one way. The weekly rate of decline, not the level. Germany's nine-point gap to its own target is the number that would get expensive in a cold snap.
  4. Tin's 4,030 tonnes. The spread doubled on an unchanged pile. Either the pile moves or the spread does.
  5. Gold either side of zero. A twelve-month return that has just crossed is the kind of thing that crosses back. Worth watching for what it says about the rate expectation underneath it, not for the number itself.
